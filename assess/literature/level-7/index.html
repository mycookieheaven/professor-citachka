Cumulative level test: Words, lines and contested texts: reading at the smallest scale and arguing about poems

Check understanding, separately from reading

12 questions · 80% to pass · no timer. This longer test revisits both units in the level. These authored questions assess recognition and application of the taught distinctions, not professional qualification. You may review the lessons and retry. Repeat attempts reuse the question bank; a remembered answer is not proof of transfer to a new situation.

Saving a lesson records reading only. Previous lesson completions have not been converted into passing scores. You can continue reading without a pass; the assessment remains unpassed.

Question 1

Two nineteenth-century reprints of a lyric silently normalise the contractions, capitals and hyphens of the original printing, while the wording of the clauses is unchanged. A student concludes that the reprints are textually identical to the original and can be quoted interchangeably. Assess the conclusion, and say what kind of personification evidence the normalisation may have removed.

Question 2

A poem suspends its main verb across five lines while the line-ends coincide with no clause boundary, and elsewhere places an object at a line-end with its agent and verb at the start of the next line. A reader claims both devices create hesitation. Which assessment best distinguishes the two? Assess the reader’s claim, name the syntactic feature that distinguishes the two devices, and state what a defender of the single-device view would have to show.

Question 3

An essay argues that a poet’s figurative language is ‘rich and suggestive’, illustrating the claim with four images from four different poems. A second reader objects that the essay describes rather than argues. What would the second reader require of the essay, and what would the first need to abandon? State precisely what the second reader would require the first essay to produce, and name the inference about imagery that the first essay would have to abandon in order to meet it.

Question 4

A student scans a poem by syllables, reports that the metre is irregular, and then claims the poet wrote free verse before free verse existed. The poem’s author published a preface describing feet of one to four syllables with one principal stress each. What does the preface change about the analysis? Explain what the preface changes about the analysis, and separate that question from the separate question whether the prefix’s claims about the rhythm’s novelty can themselves be tested.

Question 5

Two witnesses of a poem agree in the punctuation of a disputed final couplet but disagree in three earlier places, including one exclamation mark that changes an address into an exclamation. A student argues that the agreement about the couplet settles the question of who speaks the last two lines. How should that inference be qualified?

Question 6

Two sonnets were written on the same occasion, in the same form, on the same subject, and one encloses its scene inside a traveller’s report while the other narrates directly and closes by naming the poet’s own city. A critic claims the structural difference is merely a difference of technique and has no bearing on the poems’ arguments. Build the best answer to that critique.

Question 7

A critic argues that a genre has become obsolete because the knowledge it once carried is now carried by other disciplines, and predicts that its audience will dwindle to nothing. A second critic attacks one poem for relying on conventions that no longer move a reader, calling its diction harsh and its rhymes uncertain. Compare the two adverse cases in terms of what would be needed to answer each.

Question 8

An essay replies to a critic by arguing that the critic uses a key word in two senses, and then treats the dispute as settled. A second essay replies by showing what the critic’s own position presupposes. Which reply is stronger, and what does the weaker one leave undone? Decide which reply is stronger, and state what the weaker of the two leaves unassessed once the terminology has been repaired.

Question 9

You are asked to assess a dispute between a poet’s preface and a critic’s examination of that preface. The critic quotes a sentence from the preface that does not appear in the edition you have fetched, and the critic states the question at issue as whether verse admits constructions that would be alien in serious prose. What should your essay do, and in what order?

Question 10

A student’s comparative essay names a respect of difference, quotes two lines from each poem with editions identified, states an objection in the opponent’s own terms, replies with a concession, and adds a falsification clause. A reviewer says the essay is now defensible. Which residue of risk remains, and how should the essay disclose it?

Question 11

A teacher selects three poems from this level to teach the difference between a variant, a transcription artefact and an editorial normalisation. For each category, name the evidence a student would need in order to place a given difference correctly, and say what follows about the strength of any interpretive claim built on it.

Question 12

A seminar asks you to defend the proposition that a poem’s meaning includes the state of its text. Using two of this level’s poems, set out the strongest version of that proposition, the best objection to it, and the evidence that would settle the dispute in each case. Set out the strongest version of the proposition, the best objection to it, and the evidence that would settle the dispute in each of your two chosen cases.
