Cumulative level test: Prose under pressure: style as argument, and the architecture of long fiction

Check understanding, separately from reading

Question 1

Two novelists open with present-tense set pieces. Thackeray’s “Before the Curtain” places a named manager before a fair and lets him comment; Dickens opens Bleak House with mud, smoke and fog and no narrator visible at all until the chapter’s final movement. A student claims the second opening is therefore more objective. Which assessment of that claim is best?

Question 2

Johnson writes in the Preface to Shakespeare that “Nothing can please many, and please long, but just representations of general nature”, and in the Life of Cowley that the metaphysical poets’ reader “sometimes admires, is seldom pleased”. A student treats the second sentence as a change of standard. What does the repetition of the word “please” establish?

Question 3

The Gutenberg text of Gibbon’s first volume prints, at the foot of chapter XV, an editor’s note by Dean Milman which says that “the art of Gibbon” consists in “confounding together, in one undistinguishable mass, the origin and apostolic propagation of the Christian religion with its later progress”. A student quotes that note as Gibbon’s own admission. What is wrong, and what does the note actually offer?

Question 4

Paine opens Crisis No. I with “THESE are the times that try men’s souls” and, according to the editor’s preface in the Conway edition, the last Crisis of 1783 “opens with the words, ‘The times that tried men’s souls are over.’” A student reads the later sentence as Paine contradicting himself. Which reading of the pair is best?

Question 5

Hazlitt objects to Johnson’s style on the ground that “there is no discrimination, no selection, no variety in it”, while Johnson’s own Preface argues that lasting writing depends on “just representations of general nature”. A student proposes to settle the dispute by counting. Which version of that test would actually discriminate?

Question 6

Thackeray’s novel opens with a manager looking into the fair and closes with the instruction “come, children, let us shut up the box and the puppets, for our play is played out”. A student argues that the closing return proves the frame was decorative, since the story would stand without it. Which reply is strongest?

Question 7

Dickens writes that the mud is “accumulating at compound interest”, that the shops’ gas has “a haggard and unwilling look”, and that the fog reaches “the eyes and throats of ancient Greenwich pensioners”. A student concludes that the passage personifies the weather. Which account of the figures is most accurate, and most useful for an argument?

Question 8

In chapter XLII of The Portrait of a Lady the narration reports that Isabel’s suffering “was a passion of thought, of speculation, of response to every pressure”, and that Osmond had led her “into the mansion of his own habitation”, which becomes “the house of darkness, the house of dumbness, the house of suffocation”. A student asks whether these are Isabel’s words or the narrator’s. What is the best answer, and what follows for interpretation?

Question 9

Hardy writes that “‘Justice’ was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess”, and in his 1892 postscript he answers a critic by quoting Gloucester in King Lear: “As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods; / They kill us for their sport.” A student says the postscript proves the sentence is Hardy’s own view. What does the evidence actually support?

Question 10

Arnold writes that criticism must aim “to see the object as in itself it really is” and sums up its rule as “disinterestedness”; Eliot replies that criticism “must always profess an end in view” and that the critic should compose his differences with others “in the common pursuit of true judgment”. A student asks which of the two positions is falsifiable. Which answer is best?

Question 11

An essay argues that Bleak House’s opening “shows Dickens’s belief that the legal system causes all the misery of London”, and supports it with the chapter’s closing sentence about the Lord Chancellor sitting “at the very heart of the fog”. A marker objects that the quotation supports a weaker claim than the one asserted. What is the gap?

Question 12

A student’s capstone argues that Hazlitt’s familiar style is more honest than Johnson’s balanced prose, quoting “I like to go by myself” and Johnson’s “Nothing can please many, and please long”. The student’s only engagement with objection is the sentence “Some readers prefer Johnson”. Which revision turns this into a real case?
