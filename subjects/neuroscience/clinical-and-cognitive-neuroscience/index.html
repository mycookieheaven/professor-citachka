Neuroscience · Lesson 05

Clinical and Cognitive Neuroscience

Use evidence carefully: patterns guide questions, not verdicts.

I

Learning targets

By the end of this lesson, you can

  1. Explain why lesions, imaging, and behavioral tasks provide converging but limited evidence.
  2. Recognize the difference between association, mechanism, and diagnosis.
  3. Apply person-first, uncertainty-aware language to neuroscience claims.
II

Core mechanism

A brain image is evidence, not a diagnosis

Clinical neuroscience links behavior, symptoms, anatomy, development, and context. A scan can show structure or activity under specific conditions, but it rarely determines what a person thinks, feels, or should be labeled. Strong conclusions require converging evidence and appropriate expertise.

III

Illustrated scene card

Worked scene: interpreting a scan headline

A headline says a study found a brain difference between two groups.Observe → explain

Observation: The study may report an average association under one task, with overlap between individuals and no direct test of everyday functioning.

Why it matters: The responsible response is to ask about sample, task, effect size, replication, and alternative explanations before inferring cause or making claims about an individual.

Retrieval practice

Retrieve before you review

Answer aloud or on paper first. Then reveal the feedback and repair any gaps.

  1. What additional evidence can strengthen a claim beyond one imaging result?
  2. Does a group average determine an individual’s abilities or diagnosis?
  3. What is one responsible question to ask about a neuroscience headline?

What to retain

Converging behavioral, clinical, developmental, replication, and task-specific evidence can strengthen a claim. No. Group averages often overlap and do not by themselves determine an individual’s abilities or diagnosis. Ask what the study measured, who was sampled, whether the result replicates, and whether association has been mistaken for cause.
