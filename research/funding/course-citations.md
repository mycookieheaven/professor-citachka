# Open with identity, relevance and permission

A useful opening answers who you are, why this conversation could matter, and whether the owner wants to continue. A broker must not pose as the merchant’s bank, a government office or an underwriting decision-maker. The FTC’s RCG case illustrates the consequences of financing claims that contradict actual terms.[3] The scripts here are original practice, not a claim that a particular wording guarantees sales.

Fictional Harbor Bakery: “Hi, I’m Melissa with Example Funding, an independent business-financing broker. Are you considering funding for a specific project, or is now not a useful time?” If the owner says yes, ask about the project. If no, thank them and end the pitch.

# Discover the business purpose before the product

Discovery starts with the use of funds, amount needed, cash timing and repayment source. “Working capital” is too broad until you know what cash buys and how it returns. SBA materials distinguish working capital and fixed assets, and eligibility still requires lender review.[1][10] A sales diagnosis is a hypothesis to verify, not an underwriting decision.

Harbor needs an oven, while fictional Tide Catering needs supplies for a signed event. An oven can produce value over years; event inventory converts when the customer pays. Asking only “How much do you want?” hides that difference.

# Distinguish revenue, profit and spendable cash

Revenue is not the cash left after materials, wages, rent, taxes and existing obligations. A deposit may even be borrowed money or an owner transfer rather than a sale. Our underwriting method reconciles sources before judging capacity. SBA notes that most 7(a) term loans are repaid from business cash flow; a bank balance alone is not that analysis.[1]

A fictional florist has a busy holiday month but must pay its wholesaler the following week. Another shop has steady sales but large existing daily debits. Neither can safely promise that every incoming dollar is available for a new remittance.

# Create a decision map without bypassing people

A useful decision map identifies who can bind the business, who manages cash and who advises on contracts. Do not treat an accountant or co-owner as an obstacle to defeat. Document authority through the provider’s process and collect only necessary information, consistent with FTC data-minimization guidance.[7]

The shop manager likes a quote but the owner signs and the bookkeeper knows the debit calendar. A sound next step is a joint review with the owner’s permission, not asking the manager to forward a private signature link.

# Separate urgency from pressure

A merchant’s supplier deadline is a business fact to investigate. A salesperson’s desire to close is not a reason to invent scarcity. FTC financing enforcement emphasizes truthful material claims.[3] State offer and disclosure rules also matter; the New York framework addresses disclosures at a specific offer, not after a surprise signature.[2]

A bakery can confirm an equipment reservation expires Friday. A broker cannot convert that into “the government program ends today.” Ask whether a deposit, rental or delayed delivery changes the financing need.

# Qualify out without humiliating the owner

A consultative process permits “not now.” If repayment depends only on obtaining another advance, the proposal has not established a sustainable cash source. Lender standards differ; do not invent universal revenue or credit cutoffs. SBA expressly ties its lending framework to repayment ability and program eligibility.[1][10]

A fictional diner wants funding to cover repeated operating losses with no change in pricing, costs or demand. Compare that with a profitable caterer waiting on an acknowledged invoice. Both need money, but the causes require different questions.

# Turn product features into conditional relevance

A feature is what a product does; relevance is why it might fit this business. Speed, flexibility or a fixed payment can be useful but cannot erase costs. New York’s disclosures distinguish funding received, finance charges, timing and repayment; those dimensions belong in a fair comparison.[2]

“A revolving line may match recurring inventory needs, subject to availability and terms” is better than “unlimited cash whenever you want.” For a one-time machine, a term structure may be easier to evaluate than repeated short-cycle financing.

# Handle “send me information” with consent

“Send information” can mean genuine interest, lack of time or a polite no. Clarify without trapping the owner. Use approved channels and only necessary data; the FTC recommends knowing what information you hold and minimizing it.[7] A request for a brochure is not permission to submit applications everywhere.

“Certainly. Would a product overview or a side-by-side cost checklist help most? Would you prefer to contact me after reading, or schedule a review?” If the owner wants no follow-up, honor that preference.

# Respect “not interested” and stop cleanly

A clean no is a valid outcome, not a challenge to overcome. Our course’s ethical standard is to stop the pitch and record the preference through approved procedures. Calling, texting, recording and consent rules vary by channel and jurisdiction; compliance review is required before real outreach. Do not treat a business number as blanket permission.

“Understood. Thank you for letting me know. I will note your preference.” Compare “Before you go, this is your last chance,” which manufactures urgency and ignores the refusal.

# Close discovery with a teach-back summary

A discovery close is an agreement about the next investigative step, not an agreement to accept financing. Ask the owner to correct your summary. The SBA application process depends on the lender and circumstances; collecting documents is not approval.[1] Keep evidence requests narrow and secure.[7]

“You want to replace the oven, cannot disrupt payroll, and need to compare equipment financing with other options. Is that accurate? If you want, we can request the provider’s document checklist before any application.”

# MCA: receivables purchase is not a magic label

An MCA is commonly structured as a purchase of future receivables: an upfront amount for an agreed purchased amount, collected through specified remittances. The contract label does not settle its legal character. The New York Attorney General describes a case involving illegal loans disguised as MCAs; that outcome is not a judgment about every provider.[8] Classification depends on facts and jurisdiction. Refer legal interpretation to qualified counsel.

Fictional Harbor is offered a receivables purchase with estimated debits. Ask what happens when sales fall, whether reconciliation is meaningful, and what recourse applies. Do not say “not a loan, so no rules apply.”

# Business LOC: availability is not a lump-sum advance

A business line of credit permits draws under a limit and contract conditions; repayment may restore availability when it is genuinely revolving. Check renewal, draw fees, interest, collateral and freeze rights rather than assuming perpetual access. SBA’s Working Capital Pilot explicitly offers monitored lines of credit.[1] A product marketed as a line may instead make separate advances; read the agreement.

A wholesaler repeatedly buys inventory before customer collections. A monitored line may match the cycle, but a borrowing base or covenant can limit draws even when the headline limit looks large.

# Traditional term loans: repayment and asset life

A term loan advances funds with a repayment schedule and maturity. Review principal, interest, fees, collateral, guarantees and any balloon rather than judging only a monthly payment. SBA explains that most 7(a) term loans use monthly principal-and-interest payments and that variable-rate payments can change.[1] Conventional lenders set their own terms; SBA features do not automatically apply to them.

For an oven expected to serve for years, Harbor compares a conventional equipment term loan with a short remittance product. The longer term may reduce immediate strain but can increase total cost; it is not automatically preferable.

# SBA financing: lender guarantee is not owner approval

SBA’s 7(a) program provides guarantees to participating lenders; the business applies through the lender, not through a salesperson claiming to be SBA. Eligible uses include working capital, equipment and certain refinancing, subject to program and lender review.[1] A government guarantee is not a guarantee that the owner will be approved or relieved of repayment.

Fictional Ridge Manufacturing can prepare records and discuss a 7(a) option with a participating lender. A broker may assist truthfully within its role but must not call an ordinary MCA “SBA approved.”

# Equipment financing, leases and ownership

Equipment may be financed with a loan or lease. Compare who owns the asset, security, insurance, maintenance, buyout or return conditions and total cash outlay. New York’s commercial-finance rules treat lease financing as a distinct disclosure category.[2] A low lease payment does not establish ownership at the end. SBA financing also has asset-oriented alternatives.[10]

Harbor’s oven offer includes a buyout; another requires return or renegotiation. The word “lease” alone does not reveal the final cost. Ask for the option price and whether it is mandatory or elective.

# Invoice financing versus factoring

Invoice financing generally uses receivables to support borrowing; factoring involves selling receivables under a purchase agreement. New York’s rules distinguish factoring and asset-based financing.[2] Terminology varies. Verify eligible invoices, advance, reserve, fees, collection control, disputes and recourse. “Nonrecourse” is not a promise that every nonpayment or invoice dispute is covered.

Tide has an acknowledged invoice from an established customer. A factor may advance a portion, hold a reserve and collect from the customer. A disputed or unearned invoice cannot be treated as equivalent to an accepted receivable.

# HELOC: a personal home is not business collateral by default

A HELOC is an open-end line secured by home equity, not a business LOC. CFPB explains that failure to repay can result in loss of the home; rates are usually variable, and repayment after the draw period may rise substantially.[4] This course discusses the consumer product as an educational comparison, not a recommendation to pledge a home for business distress. Actual purpose and legal coverage require specialist review.

Harbor’s owner asks about using a HELOC to rescue payroll. The business might receive cash, but the owner introduces household housing risk. Do not conceal that transfer of risk behind “lower rate.”

# Credit repair: legitimate disputes, not invented identities

Credit repair companies cannot legally remove accurate, timely negative information; consumers can dispute errors themselves.[5] FTC warns against false identity-theft reports, false applications and promises of a new credit identity.[6] Never recommend a CPN, substituting an EIN for personal identity, or hiding credit history. No sales script can guarantee a score increase or funding approval.

A fictional owner identifies a payment reported late despite a dated receipt. They can document and dispute the error. A different owner has an accurate recent delinquency; calling it identity theft would be false.

# Consolidation: payoff, payment and total cost

A consolidation loan pays off separate obligations and replaces them with a new one. CFPB cautions that term length, fees and costs can make the total paid higher even when payments fall.[5] Its guidance is consumer-facing; the arithmetic lesson transfers to business comparisons, not a claim that all consumer protections govern commercial deals. Confirm payoff quotes and closure rather than assuming old debits stop.

Harbor compares keeping its existing schedule against a longer replacement loan. The replacement reduces weekly strain but extends obligations. Both total outlay and the lowest cash balance matter.

# Reverse consolidation: interrogate the marketing term

“Reverse consolidation” is a nonstandard marketing label, not a uniform product definition established by the primary authorities retrieved for this course. One industry explainer describes recurring deposits that help meet existing MCA debits while adding a new obligation; old positions remain in place.[9] That is evidence of marketing usage, not regulatory endorsement or a guarantee of how every offer works. Inspect each contract and disbursement schedule.

An owner hears “one smaller payment,” but old providers still debit the bank account and the new funder sends conditional weekly deposits. This can be layered funding rather than a payoff. Ask what happens if a deposit is delayed or terminated.

# Gross advance, net proceeds and purchased amount

For this fictional fixed-factor illustration, purchased amount = gross advance × factor. Net proceeds = gross advance − deducted fees. Difference between purchased amount and net proceeds is a useful dollar comparison, not an APR or automatically the legally defined finance charge. New York requires applicable offer disclosures; actual classification of charges follows the rule, not our teaching shortcut.[2]

Harbor: gross $50,000, factor 1.3, fees $2,500. Purchased amount = $50,000 × 1.3 = $65,000. Net = $50,000 − $2,500 = $47,500. Purchased minus net = $17,500. No other costs are assumed in this illustration.

# Factor rate is not APR

A factor multiplies a funded base to obtain an agreed amount in our illustration. APR is an annualized cost measure requiring amounts, fees and timing. NY DFS explicitly addresses finance-charge and APR calculation.[2] Do not call a factor “interest,” subtract one and label the result APR, or annualize a fixed fee by guessing a duration. A compliant estimate is the provider’s responsibility under applicable rules.

Harbor’s 1.3 factor produces $65,000 on $50,000 before accounting for deducted fees. The same purchased amount collected more quickly has different annualized economics. We deliberately do not invent an APR from an incomplete schedule.

# Daily remittances and the calendar

Daily, business-day and calendar-day schedules are not interchangeable. Check holidays, start date and how failed payments are handled. Our example assumes exactly one remittance on each specified debit day, not a legal promise of a fixed term. Actual sales-based duration may change. State disclosure rules address payment frequency and estimated duration.[2]

Assume $65,000 over 100 scheduled debit days: $650 per day. Five debit days = $3,250; a 20-debit-day month = $13,000, while 23 debit days = $14,950. These are calendar scenarios, not universal monthly equivalents.

# Revenue share and reconciliation are different mechanisms

A true percentage collection varies with the defined revenue base. A fixed estimated debit may require a reconciliation procedure to align collections with sales. Read eligibility, evidence, deadlines, frequency and refund/adjustment mechanics. Do not promise automatic relief when the contract requires a request. Legal treatment depends on facts, not just whether “reconciliation” appears on the page.[8]

Illustrative share: 10% of $4,000 eligible daily receipts = $400; 10% of $3,000 = $300. A fixed $650 debit does not automatically become either amount. The agreement’s revenue definition and adjustment process control the actual transaction.

# Cash-flow headroom under a sales shock

Headroom is what remains after included operating outflows and the proposed obligation. It is not a lender approval threshold. State what is included and test lower receipts without pretending all expenses fall proportionately. Tax reserves, owner draws, seasonality and other liabilities still need verification. A positive average does not establish day-by-day solvency.

Fictional weekly receipts $12,000 minus operating outflows $8,500 = $3,500 before financing. Less $3,250 remittance leaves $250. If receipts fall to $10,800 and costs stay fixed, room is $2,300 and residual is -$950. No other obligations assumed; add them if present.

# Consolidation arithmetic: relief can cost more

Compare future cash outflows from today, not the original historical cost of the old borrowing. Include payoff fees and financing deductions without double counting amounts already included. CFPB warns that lower payments over a longer term can cost more.[5] This arithmetic illustration is not an offer or a regulatory APR calculation.

Old schedule: $3,000 weekly × 12 remaining weeks = $36,000. Replacement: $1,500 × 28 weeks = $42,000, assuming it fully retires the old obligations and no separate fees. Weekly relief = $1,500; extra total future outlay = $6,000.

# Layered funding: count deposits and every debit

For a layered arrangement, net bank outflow = all old debits + new debits − new disbursements in that period. New disbursements are financing inflows, not operating revenue or forgiven balances. The industry source describes this marketing usage but does not establish a uniform contract.[9] Test the loss of a promised inflow while obligations remain.

Fictional weekly old debits $7,000 plus new debit $4,000 = gross outflow $11,000. With a $6,500 new deposit, net outflow is $4,500. If that deposit stops while debits remain, outflow is $11,000. This is a one-period stress illustration, not total cost.

# Invoice reserves: reconcile the second cash receipt

Invoice face value is not necessarily immediate usable cash. In this fictional factoring example, the reserve is released after full customer payment, less the stated fee. Real agreements may impose other fees, recourse or dispute adjustments; verify those rather than treating this illustration as standard pricing. Factoring has its own commercial disclosure category in New York.[2]

Invoice $20,000; advance 80% = $16,000. One assumed fee of 3% of invoice face = $600. Reserve release = $20,000 − $16,000 − $600 = $3,400. Total received = $19,400, assuming full collection and no other charges.

# LOC availability and interest: state the convention

A simplified revolving availability calculation is limit minus outstanding balance, before reserves or restrictions. Simple interest needs a balance, annual nominal rate, day count and convention; fees and compounding can change actual cost. Do not call that calculation APR. Verify the lender’s documents and applicable disclosures.[2]

Example A: limit $50,000 less drawn $22,000 = $28,000 before restrictions. Separate Example B: a constant $24,000 balance at 12% nominal annual rate for 30 days on an actual/360 simple-interest convention gives $240. No fees, compounding or balance changes assumed.

# Balloon and equipment total-outlay audit

A payment schedule must include the final payment and upfront amounts. These examples isolate arithmetic; they are not amortization quotes, market prices or advice. Lease classification, ownership and disclosure rules require the actual agreement.[2] Never assume a future refinance will cover the final balance.

Example A assumes $30,000 principal, no interest or fees, and 20 principal payments of $500: remaining balloon $20,000. Example B: equipment lease cash outlay = $10,000 upfront + 48 payments of $1,000 + $2,000 buyout = $60,000, before tax, insurance or maintenance.

# Build an underwriting file without rewriting reality

A useful file may include bank statements, financial statements, tax returns, debt schedules, receivable/payable aging and ownership records as the provider requires. SBA emphasizes that documents vary with lender and loan circumstances, and its working-capital program calls for timely financial and aging reports.[1] Do not alter statements, conceal liabilities or manufacture revenue to make a file pass.

Ridge’s deposits exceed reported sales because an owner transfer was included. Flag and document the difference. A catering invoice is still disputed; label it as such rather than removing the dispute from an aging report.

# Consent, secure channels and minimum necessary data

FTC’s security guidance calls for inventorying data, keeping only what is needed, protecting it and disposing of it appropriately.[7] In real work, confirm applicable privacy, credit-report authorization and retention obligations with compliance; a casual conversation is not blanket consent to circulate a file. Never ask for passwords, bank login codes or sensitive records in this course.

An owner offers to text a bank password. Decline and direct them to the provider’s verified secure process. If considering another funder, explain the destination and obtain the appropriate authorization first; do not “shotgun” submissions.

# Personal guarantees and UCC security interests

A personal guarantee and a security interest are different: one may create personal liability under its wording; the other concerns specified collateral. A UCC filing can give public notice of a claimed security interest, but its scope and effect need the agreement and legal review. NY’s Yellowstone materials discuss guarantors and UCC liens; FTC’s RCG case involved misleading guarantee claims.[8][3] “Unsecured” does not automatically mean “no personal liability.”

An offer says no real-estate collateral, yet includes a personal guaranty and business-asset security agreement. Those are not contradictions in every case, but they are material terms the owner must understand, not features to hide.

# Existing advances, stacking and payoff verification

Stacking means layering additional financing on existing obligations. Contract restrictions, lien priorities and required consents vary; never claim a universal right to add a position. Assemble balances, remittances, payoff quotes, guarantees and restrictions, then obtain provider and legal review where needed. A disclosed schedule is also essential to avoid the hidden-fee and collection problems illustrated in enforcement records.[3][8]

Harbor has a daily advance and a secured bank line. A new offer may conflict with covenants or collateral rights even if each payment seems small. A buyout quote needs written payoff amounts and confirmation of releases; estimated balances are not enough.

# Build a comparable offer sheet

Use one comparison frame: purpose, gross and net funding, all costs, total remittance, dated payments, variability, collateral, guarantees, early payoff, default and broker compensation. Applicable state disclosures may prescribe more specific forms; a salesperson’s sheet does not replace them.[2] FTC enforcement underscores the risk of undisclosed deductions and misleading terms.[3]

Offer A gives more headline cash but subtracts fees. Offer B has a smaller headline and a balloon. Put net proceeds and the complete dated schedules beside each other before calling either “cheaper.” Mark unknowns instead of silently using zeros.

# Handle “the rate is too high” without deflection

A price objection may concern total cost, annualized cost or cash burden. Ask which, but do not use the question to dodge the expense. Factor rate is not APR. Review applicable disclosures and compare suitable alternatives; a high-cost product does not become affordable because approval is fast.[2] Do not promise savings unsupported by a complete comparison.

“That is a reasonable concern. Is the main problem the total dollars, how quickly payments leave, or both? Let us compare a smaller amount, a different product and waiting, including their limitations.”

# Welcome the accountant review

An accountant can help assess cash flow, tax effects and record quality; qualified counsel handles legal interpretation. Do not portray independent advice as lost momentum. The CFPB’s consolidation guidance illustrates why payment reduction alone is insufficient.[5] A good sales handoff delivers the facts needed for review without asserting that professional approval is assured.

“Please involve your accountant. With your permission I can send the written offer, net proceeds, fees and payment calendar through the approved channel. What questions should we resolve before you decide?”

# Ask for an informed next step, not a pressured signature

A consultative close tests understanding, unresolved concerns and the owner’s chosen next step. It does not use a false binary between signing now and business failure. Review actual terms and required disclosures first.[2] Do not call an application “just information” if it authorizes a credit inquiry or distribution of data; describe the actual consent.

“You have the written costs and schedule. What remains unclear? If this fits after your review, would you like to proceed through the provider’s application process, compare another option, or pause?”

# Funding-day verification and post-funding support

A deal is not operationally complete when someone says “funded.” Confirm the actual net deposit, itemized deductions, payment start, servicing contact and any payoff completion through approved records. FTC’s RCG case illustrates why misleading costs and collection behavior matter.[3] If a discrepancy appears, record it and escalate; do not pressure the owner into silence.

Harbor receives less cash than the written net proceeds. Compare the offer and settlement record, ask for the itemization, and route the issue to the provider. If replacing an old obligation, verify payoff and stop-debit arrangements instead of assuming them.

# Renewals are new decisions, not automatic wins

A renewal may retire an old balance and add new money, but its headline amount can exaggerate usable new cash. Re-underwrite current cash flow and disclose fees and payoff mechanics. Early payoff does not necessarily remove fixed charges; actual contracts and applicable disclosures control.[2] Do not teach a renewal cycle as guaranteed access or as proof of business health.

Harbor is invited to renew while sales weaken. Before discussing an increased advance, separate old payoff from net new funds and compare keeping the current schedule. A smaller apparent daily debit may hide a longer or more expensive commitment.

# Coverage ratios: numerator discipline and downside

For this exercise only, coverage = defined cash available for debt service ÷ scheduled debt service over the same period. Lenders may define both differently; there is no universal pass mark here. Normalize unusual items, account for required outflows and test downside. A ratio cannot fix misstated inputs or reveal every intra-period cash trough.

Ridge has $9,000 defined monthly available cash and $6,000 scheduled service: coverage 1.50×. If available cash drops to $5,400, coverage is 0.90×. The second numerator is below service. Neither number is an approval decision or a substitute for a dated cash forecast.

# A weekly forecast can hide a midweek failure

A rolling cash forecast tracks opening unrestricted cash, dated operating receipts, financing inflows and all outflows. Keep financing separate from sales and record the minimum balance. Timing can make an otherwise profitable project unfinanceable on the proposed schedule. Our method is a training framework, not an accounting opinion.

Tide opens Monday with $2,000 and payroll of $3,000 leaves before a customer pays Friday. Monday’s balance becomes -$1,000 before that collection. A positive Friday balance cannot retrospectively cover Monday’s gap. Verify actual settlement dates and any available, permitted buffer.

# Renewal incremental economics without double counting

Compare the renewal with a clearly defined counterfactual: continuing the old schedule. Separate old payoff, new fees and cash delivered. Incremental future cost is not APR and does not capture timing or all contingent fees. A valid comparison requires the actual payoff quote and early-payment terms.[2]

Assume old remaining scheduled outflow and payoff both equal $20,000. New gross $40,000, fees $2,500, old payoff $20,000: net new cash $17,500. New purchased amount $40,000 × 1.3 = $52,000. New total − old remaining − net new cash = $14,500 incremental dollar cost, with no other charges.

# Early payoff and prepayment: audit the exact wording

Some arrangements use fixed charges rather than daily accruing interest; early payoff benefits depend on the agreement and applicable disclosure. Do not say “no prepayment penalty” means “all unearned cost disappears.” New York’s commercial framework addresses disclosure of financing costs.[2] Obtain a dated payoff statement and compare it with remaining scheduled outflow.

Harbor hears “no penalty,” yet its quote still includes the unpaid fixed purchased amount. Ask whether any discount is contractual, discretionary or unavailable, and whether the deadline changes the amount. Avoid inventing a legal entitlement to a rebate.

# Multi-product recommendation memo with a no-deal option

An advanced comparison starts with constraints, not commission. State the use, time to cash, available evidence and risks. Consider term/SBA, a business LOC, equipment or invoice structures only when relevant; do not imply qualification. SBA offers different funding paths, and New York distinguishes multiple commercial financing categories.[10][2] A home-secured alternative introduces separate household risk.[4]

Ridge needs a machine and recurring materials. A machine-specific term structure and a monitored working-capital line solve different problems. Combining them also changes aggregate service and collateral, so independent feasibility of each does not prove feasibility together.

# State-specific disclosures and role boundaries

The United States has no single sales script that establishes compliance for every commercial-financing transaction. New York DFS’s Part 600 implements a state commercial-disclosure framework with product categories and broker/provider duties.[2] Do not extrapolate its coverage to every state, amount or entity. Confirm current applicability, exemptions, registration, disclosures, solicitation and recording rules with qualified compliance personnel.

The same broker speaks with owners in different states about sales-based financing. A disclosure prepared for one offer and jurisdiction cannot simply be relabeled for the other. Product classification and provider roles also matter.

# Distress triage: do not finance an unexplained rescue

A merchant facing missed obligations needs accurate information and appropriate professional support, not a promise that more funding fixes everything. CFPB distinguishes counseling, consolidation, settlement and credit repair in the consumer context.[5] Do not transfer every consumer protection to business debt or promise negotiated reductions. Business restructuring and insolvency questions belong with qualified counsel and advisers.

A fictional restaurant is behind on taxes, payroll and several advances. A delayed receivable may be one issue, but layering another debit without a viability review can compound the problem. Ask about actual cash and existing provider communication; do not direct the owner to stop paying.

# Reverse-consolidation diligence: prove the exit

A temporary net-outflow reduction is not an exit plan. For a proposal marketed as reverse consolidation, trace old balances, new funding commitments, debit priority, fees, termination conditions and the remaining tail after deposits end. The industry description supports only the existence of this marketed structure; it is not authoritative evidence of safety or uniform regulation.[9]

A fictional proposal shows attractive first-week net cash, but the old debits continue and the new provider can suspend deposits after a covenant breach. The merchant still owes obligations. Test a late deposit, early suspension and a slow-sales case before calling the arrangement relief.

# Advanced roleplay: price, accountant and existing advances

Complex objections are often valid constraints, not resistance to beat. Combine accurate cost language, an obligations map and independent review. FTC enforcement supplies a reason to keep sales statements consistent with actual terms.[3] Our roleplay assesses the reasoning you articulate, not persuasiveness inferred by an AI; the model and checklist are self-review aids.

Harbor: “The factor is high, I have two advances and my accountant dislikes daily payments.” A strong response acknowledges all three, asks permission to compare, and leaves open the possibility that no new financing is suitable. Do not answer only the easiest objection.

# Capstone: defend a recommendation and the decision to decline

This capstone integrates the course, not professional certification. A defensible memo states the purpose, verified facts, assumptions, dated cash flows, alternatives, costs, legal-review flags and conditions that would reverse the recommendation. Distinguish facts from estimates. Regulatory and consumer sources here have different scopes; none endorses a particular merchant transaction.[1][4][8]

Fictional Ridge wants a machine, carries an existing secured line, has seasonal receipts and is offered layered funding plus a personal HELOC. Separate machine finance from working capital, investigate lien restrictions, reject “HELOC equals business LOC,” and stress the full payment schedule. A documented pause is a successful result if evidence is insufficient.
